A hypothetical example, but you may recognize it. At a distributor with about thirty employees, orders come in through the sales reps’ WhatsApp, become a row in a shared spreadsheet and wait for someone in finance to approve the discount before going to the warehouse. The sales coordinator knows every bottleneck along that path, because she spends her days fixing them. One weekend, she describes the flow to an AI tool and comes out with an app: the rep enters the order, finance approves it with one click and the warehouse sees the day’s queue.

On Monday she shows it to the team, and it works. Two weeks later, nobody opens the spreadsheet anymore.

Nothing broke, and I’m not going to make up a disaster to give the story a moral. What interests me is something else: somewhere between the demo and the second week, the app stopped being an experiment and became the way the company sells. Nobody decided that. It happened because the tool was good.

## What we’re talking about

This story lands in the middle of a noisy argument. On one side, people saying anyone can now build software by talking to an AI and that much of traditional development has turned into bureaucracy. On the other, developers who look at these apps and see a queue of problems waiting to happen.

The term of the moment is vibe coding. It showed up in a [post by Andrej Karpathy](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1886192184808149383) in February 2025, describing a way of programming where you accept whatever the AI generates without reading the code and steer by the result. Karpathy himself said it was fine for throwaway weekend projects. The term has since spread and picked up different meanings, so here is how I use it: vibe coding is generating and changing software through instructions, without necessarily understanding or checking how it was implemented. A developer using AI and then reviewing, testing and validating what it produced is something else, a distinction Simon Willison makes well [in a post from the same year](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Mar/19/vibe-coding/). I use Claude Code and Codex every day, and whatever goes live goes through me first.

## What each side sees clearly, and what it misses

The skeptical developers are right about a lot. Security, maintenance and reliability are what separate a tool that helps from one that leaks customer data or loses an order. But there comes a point where that rigor turns into reflex. When the answer to any idea is “this needs a proper architecture”, when the proposed solution is bigger than the problem, or when whoever built the app is treated as someone who doesn’t know what they’re doing, the debate loses exactly the person who understands the process best. And one detail tends to get left out: hand-written code has bugs too. What makes a system reliable is the decisions, the controls and the checking, not who typed each line.

On the other side, business owners see something many developers don’t: the pain. The coordinator knew exactly where orders got stuck, which discounts needed approval and what the warehouse needed to see first. AI made it much cheaper to turn that knowledge into a tool you can actually try out, and that has real value, including for people who will never program. What’s easy to underestimate has nothing to do with intelligence: a working screen only shows the part of the solution that’s in plain sight.

## What the screen doesn’t show

Back to the distributor. The app works, but there are questions the demo doesn’t answer.

Can a sales rep see another rep’s orders and customers? If so, that can turn into a commission dispute or a customer being contacted twice. The app sends approved orders to the system that issues invoices. If that integration fails halfway, or runs twice, does anyone notice before the customer gets billed twice? If an order is deleted by mistake, can it be recovered, or did it only exist there? A month from now, when the coordinator asks the AI to change the shipping rule, how will she know the discount calculation that used to work still works? And the code, the account where the app runs and the passwords: whose name are they in? If they’re tied to her personal email and she leaves the company, the company keeps the process and loses the tool.

None of these questions assume the app is wrong. They gain weight once other people depend on it. While it was the coordinator testing with fake orders, a failure cost an afternoon. Now it costs sales, money and customer trust.

## Experiment or operations

That’s why I don’t think every AI experiment needs a professional around. A prototype with made-up data, used by one person, that can be rebuilt in a day if it breaks, can be done without asking anyone for permission. That’s how you find out whether an idea is any good.

The care should grow with the damage a failure can do. Some signs a tool has changed category: other people depend on it to do their work; it stores real customer data or financial information; employees with different roles access the same things; it writes to other systems instead of only reading from them; nobody could rebuild it quickly if it disappeared. The more of these signs show up, the more expensive it gets to have no one looking inside.

## A middle path, nothing new about it

The polarization hides an arrangement that already works in plenty of places. The company brings its knowledge of the business and its priorities. AI speeds up experimentation and development. And someone technical understands the problem, weighs the alternatives, leads the build, reviews, validates and looks after the tool afterwards. Calling that a methodology would be a stretch: it’s the same old software development, now with a much faster tool in hand.

In this arrangement, the technical role goes beyond policing generated code, and it starts before any line is written: does the problem deserve a system? Does an off-the-shelf tool solve it? Is it enough to connect two systems the company already has? At the distributor, one possible answer is that the management system it already pays for has an order module with approvals that was never set up. Then the best decision is not to maintain new software at all: configure what exists, maybe build a small integration, and retire the app. Even so, the app wasn’t wasted, because it described the process more precisely than any meeting would have. And when the existing system has no API, there are still ways to integrate without rewriting everything, as I wrote about <a href={publicationUrl('en', 'article', 'interface-como-integracao')}>in another article</a>.

When building does make sense, the work continues during the build, using AI to go faster without giving up on understanding what goes live, and it carries on after delivery, because the shipping rule changes, order volume grows and the invoicing system gets a new version. Automated tests (small programs that check that what worked still works), backups someone has actually tried restoring and access split by role come in proportion to the risk, not because they’re on a checklist.

And it’s worth being honest about the limits of all this. Having a professional involved doesn’t eliminate failures, and it doesn’t make every project viable. What changes is the quality of the decisions, the fact that things get checked, and having a named person responsible for how the tool evolves. For many companies, that’s what separates an app everyone uses while hoping nothing goes wrong from a system the company knows how to look after.

This reasoning is what guides my work as a <a href={pathFor('en', 'devPartner')}>Dev partner</a>.
