If you follow the topic, you’ve seen both camps. One shows off apps built in an afternoon with AI and announces that paying developers is a thing of the past. The other answers with security holes, code nobody can maintain and the prediction that, sooner or later, someone will have to throw it all away and start over. Both are loud, and each has a piece of the truth.

Caught in the middle is the person who most needs an answer: the owner or manager of a small or mid-sized business, with a process that eats hours of the team’s time, systems that don’t talk to each other and an idea that has been sitting in a drawer for two years. For that person, the debate arrives as a choice between two paths. Either hire a development project that feels too big for the need, or open an AI tool and try to solve everything on your own.

<FalseChoice lang="en" />

I think that choice is false, and I think it hurts exactly the companies with the most to gain from AI. There is a third way, nothing new about it, and for many companies it makes more sense than either extreme.

First, a note on terms. In this piece, vibe coding means creating and changing software by giving instructions to an AI, without necessarily understanding or checking the code it produces. The term showed up in a [post by Andrej Karpathy](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1886192184808149383) in 2025 and has since picked up other meanings, but that’s the one I use here. Old-school software means development led by someone who understands what is being built, with or without AI in hand.

## Both sides are right, up to a point

Developers who criticize vibe coding aren’t making up problems. An app that works fine in a demo can show one customer’s data to another, charge twice when an integration repeats itself, or get so tangled that every change breaks something else. It happens, and anyone who has had to maintain a system like that knows how much work it is.

The trouble is the prescription that usually comes with it: “hire a developer and do it properly.” For a fifteen-person company, that line ignores almost everything that matters. There’s no tech team, no room to wait months to automate a report, and the proposals that come in are often designed for much bigger companies. When the only “right” path fits only other people’s budgets, it’s no surprise the owner opens an AI tool on a Sunday night and tries it alone.

On the other side, the business owner using AI to get an idea off the ground is doing something valuable. They know the pain better than any outside developer: where orders get stuck, which spreadsheet nobody is allowed to delete, what customers complain about every week. Being able to turn that knowledge into a tool without depending on a team the company doesn’t even have is a real change.

But knowing the business and being able to ask for a tool isn’t the same as knowing what it takes to build one. Where the data lives, who can see what, what happens when something fails, how to change it six months from now without breaking what already works. This has nothing to do with intelligence. It’s a different craft, the same way a developer doesn’t know off the top of their head the margin on each of the company’s products.

It’s also worth saying that this polarization doesn’t describe the whole market. Traditional development isn’t a synonym for slow, expensive or anti-AI, and plenty of good people already work differently. The binary choice lives more in the debate than in practice. The problem is that the debate is what reaches the business owner.

## The third way

For a small or mid-sized business, arguing over whether the code will be written by a person or by an AI gets you little. The more useful question is a different one: who is going to lead the work?

The third way is working with a specialist who actively uses AI to understand, define and build solutions together with the business. They don’t show up only at the end, to review generated code or fix whatever went wrong. They take part from the moment the problem is defined, choose the path, lead the build and look after how the solution evolves. The business owner brings knowledge of the business and its priorities. The specialist brings technical judgment and the ability to execute. And AI extends what that professional can produce.

<ThirdWay lang="en" />

None of this is my invention, and plenty of people already work this way. What I argue is that this arrangement should be the first option a small business considers, not an alternative hidden between two extremes. AI made the very part that made development feel too big, the execution, much cheaper. What it doesn’t solve on its own is the part the owner can’t take on, which is the technical decisions. Put the two together and the company gets the pace AI allows without betting its processes on choices it can’t evaluate.

## How it works in practice

<PracticeLoop lang="en" />

It starts by understanding the pain before generating any screen. Many companies come asking for “a system” when the real problem is that two people type the same information into different places, or that a report depends on someone who knows where to click. Generating screens is fast; figuring out what’s worth generating is what actually saves time.

Then comes the decision about how to solve it, and it doesn’t always end in new code. Systems that don’t talk to each other call for an integration, not a third system. A report assembled by hand every Monday can become a small automation. An internal tracking tool might be replaced by an off-the-shelf product that costs less than maintaining something of your own. And that idea that never left the drawer, a portal where customers follow their own orders, for example, may well deserve to be built, starting with the smallest version that already helps someone.

When it’s time to build, AI comes in strong. It writes code and tests, helps make sense of an old system and clears away the most tedious part of the work. That makes it possible to show a working version early and adjust it with the people who will use it, instead of presenting everything finished at the end.

Technical knowledge shows up in the decisions and in the validation. Security, maintenance and reliability come in here as criteria for choosing: who can access what, how the integration behaves if it runs twice, how to change one rule without breaking another. These decisions are cheap at the start and expensive when someone discovers the problem later. That’s where a specialist earns their keep, far more than in the amount of code they type.

And the solution keeps evolving. The company’s priorities shift, volume grows, a new need appears. Whoever led the build knows what was done and can move on to the next priority without starting from scratch.

## The specialist has to change too

This arrangement asks something of developers. There’s no point using AI to speed up the code while keeping everything else the old way: bloated scope, architecture bigger than the problem, months of discovery before showing anything, and distance from the people who use the tool every day. If the developer just gets faster at producing the same oversized project, the company still gains nothing.

<FasterNotCloser lang="en" />

It also means respecting what the owner has already done. A prototype built with AI by someone who knows the process can be the best description of the need a specialist will ever get, often worth more than a requirements meeting. Treating it as trash to throw away wastes exactly the knowledge that was missing.

And it’s worth being honest: having a specialist lead the work doesn’t guarantee nothing will fail, and it doesn’t make every idea viable. What changes is the quality of the decisions, the fact that things get checked, and having someone responsible for how what was built evolves.
